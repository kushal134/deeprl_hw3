\end{answerbox}
\boxlabel{Reflection: clipping vs.\ KL penalty}
\begin{answerbox}{6.5cm}
Clipping in our trial was more stable in terms of avoiding large KL divergences and large loss spikes. The KL run had a late KL spike above $0.5$, as well as sharp increases in policy and value losses while clipping remained relatively stabler.  Clipping was also more stable in the sense that it reached a higher MA(50) of $142.3$ while KL later collapsed to $-104.5$. \emph{However,} KL's best checkpoint evaluated better at $240.1$ compared to clipping's $214.9$.

Clipping's adv. are simplicity and fewer large KL and loss spikes and better retention of training performance, while its disadv. is that it only removes incentives for excessive probability changes and does not directly constrain overall policy divergence. The KL penalty explicitly discourages divergence and produced a better best checkpoint in our run, but requires adapting $\beta$. If the penalty is too weak, disruptive updates can occur and if its too strong, learning can slow. \emph{Neither approach guarantees small updates}.
\end{answerbox}

Final Performance: $254.2\pm 57.0$.\par
\emph{Training:} Ending MA(50) = $245.3$. Training returns (max/mean/min): all episodes $311.5/2.8/-614.8$; last 50 $302.6/245.3/15.3$ (SD $53.9$).\par
\end{answerbox}
\boxlabel{Discussion}
\begin{answerbox}{6.5cm}
With $\epsilon=0.3$, learning was faster than 1.4.1 and the ending MA(50) increased from $142.3$ to $245.3$, and best-checkpoint evaluation increased from $214.9$ to $254.2$. KL was generally around $0.01$, compared with roughly $0.005$ for the 1.4.1, so there were larger policy changes. Lastly, last-50 return SD was similar ($53.9$ versus $51.5$).

For positive advantages, clipping removes the incentive to increase $r_t$ beyond $1+\epsilon$ and for negative advantages, it removes the incentive to decrease $r_t$ below $1-\epsilon$. Larger $\epsilon$ therefore allows stronger changes before clipping comes in, making the realized objective closer to the unclipped policy gradients. 

The trade-off is faster learning versus potentially less stable updates in terms of loss spikes and KL divergences. In our case, the run benefited from the larger threshold while remaining relatively stable.
\end{answerbox}

\end{answerbox}
\boxlabel{Discussion}
\begin{answerbox}{6.5cm}
\small{
Full replay performed worse than 1.4.1 with the ending MA(50) being $-6.5$ as compared to $142.3$, and the best checkpoint evaluation was $-113.3$ while 1.4.1 was $214.9$. Losses decreased relatively smoothly, and last-50 return SD was only $25.1$. However, KL remained around $0.01$ with repeated spikes (higher than $0.05$ in 1.4.1). Older trajectories were collected by policies that chose different actions and visited different states. Their stored advantages and value targets may therefore be less relevant to the current policy. The probability ratio accounts for changes in action probabilities at each sampled state, but does not account for how often the current policy visits that state (\textbf{state visitation}). So, replay can direct updates toward states that matter less to the current policy.

Using \emph{more recent trajectories} and recomputing advantages and value targets with some off policy corrections could improve learning.
}

Final performance: 253.0 ± 44.6

\boxlabel{Discussion}
\begin{answerbox}{6.5cm}
\fontsize{11.5}{13}\selectfont
From the plots, we observed that removing target policy smoothing made learning slower and less stable. With \(\sigma=0.2\), the evaluation return increased steadily  reaching about 250 by 300k steps and then plateauing around 270, with final return of \(275.3 \pm 18.8\). With \(\sigma=0\), the agent stayed around \(-60\) to \(-100\) for the first 250k steps and then improved more erratically, including a drop from about 175 at 400k steps to 85 at 450k. Its final return was \(253.0 \pm 44.6\). Importantly, it had more than twice the spread across evaluation episodes.

The critic also showed a similar pattern. Q values are smoother with target policy smoothing whereas without it they are less consistent and noisier. This also matches the purpose of TD3's target smoothing. The noise which was added averages out the target Q value over the nearby actions hence, making it harder for the actor to exploit narrow Q function peaks. Overall, the results do suggest that smoothing does improve learning speed and stability rather than clearly improving final performance.
\end{answerbox}

\begin{answerbox}{3.2cm}
\texttt{policy delay = 1}: Final performance: 194.2 ± 69.8 \par
\texttt{policy delay = 4}: Final performance: 263.8 ± 20.9
\end{answerbox}
\boxlabel{Discussion}

\begin{answerbox}{8cm}
\texttt{policy\_delay} controls how often the actor and target networks are
updated relative to the critics. A larger delay means each actor update uses a
more accurate critic, but the actor gets fewer updates and the targets track the
online networks more slowly. This is a tradeoff between noisy updates and slow
learning.

Final evaluation returns were $\approx 195$ (delay 1), $\approx 270$ (delay 2,
baseline), and $\approx 264$ (delay 4). With delay 1, the actor exploits the
critic's errors after every update, so learning was noisy: there was a large
early $Q$ dip, a long reward plateau, and non-monotonic evaluation returns.

Delay 4
trained more smoothly. We expected it to learn more slowly, but this did not
happen for this seed, since the actor still received about 125k effective updates (500k/2). The
baseline performed best and we think ite because it kept most of the stability benefit while still
updating often enough to learn quickly, which matches the TD3 paper's choice of
$d = 2$.

We observed large seed-to-seed variance: in some seeds neither setting learned
well, and in others only one did.
\end{answerbox}

\boxlabel{Analysis: sample efficiency vs.\ stability vs.\ wall-clock}
\begin{answerbox}{8cm}
\emph{Sample efficiency.} UTD $N$ performs $N$ gradient updates per environment step, reusing collected data more often. Using the same budget, MA(50) is $225.6$ for the UTD $1$, $242.2$ for UTD $2$, and $0.5$ for UTD $4$. So, UTD $2$ improves sample efficiency in this run, but more updates do not help UTD $4$.\par

\emph{Stability.} UTD $2$ has large early critic-loss spikes, but these decrease as the policy learns high returns, while occasional poor episodes remain (last-50 SD $113.2$). UTD $4$ also has early loss spikes, then settles near zero return. Its smaller return SD ($21.5$) and low late critic losses describe consistently poor behavior, rather than successful learning. \emph{We think what happened is that more/repeated replay updates amplified errors in bootstrapped targets.}\par

\emph{Wall-clock.} UTD $2$ takes $27.7$ minutes and UTD $4$ takes $41.7$ minutes for the same number of steps. The baseline takes $35.3$ minutes for $500$k steps, so its longer environment interaction budget must be considered. UTD $2$ offers the best observed tradeoff, while UTD $4$ costs more without improving returns. Results on MacBook Air 2026 with Apple M5 \& 24GB memory, and run in parallel.
\end{answerbox}
